Tools that made people powerful

From the stone flake to the spreadsheet, the same thing keeps happening: a tool lets one person do what used to take many, or what nobody could do at all.

·Vlad Pazych·5 min

Some of the earliest known stone tools, found at Gona in Ethiopia, are about 2.6 million years old. They are sharp flakes struck from river cobbles. With them, their makers could cut meat and break bones for marrow, food that hands and teeth alone could not reach.

When the Domesday Book surveyed England in 1086, it recorded more than five thousand water mills. Each one took over grinding that had been hours of daily work at a hand quern, and gave those hours back to the village.

Printing

Around 1450 in Mainz, Johannes Gutenberg brought together movable metal type, an oil-based ink and a screw press. Within fifty years, presses were running in more than two hundred European towns, and millions of books had been printed. Reading stopped being the work of copyists and became something anyone could own.

Give me a place to stand, and I will move the earth. — attributed to Archimedes, on the lever

In 1979 Dan Bricklin and Bob Frankston released VisiCalc for the Apple II. A financial model that took days to recalculate by hand now updated the moment one number changed. Formulas started with an @, as in @SUM, and many people bought the computer just to run it.

  • c. 2.6 million years ago: stone flakes, Gona
  • 1086: more than five thousand water mills in the Domesday Book
  • c. 1450: movable type, Mainz
  • 1979: VisiCalc on the Apple II

None of these tools replaced the person using it. Each one made that person able to do more. More at alanremarc.com.
